## 7. Professional Sourcing — Validating the Approach Against Existing Research

Before finalizing design choices, checked whether professionals in vocational psychology already study this exact territory. They do — key sources and takeaways below.

### 7.1 O*NET Interest Profiler (US Dept. of Labor) — reference model for item-writing
- Free, public-domain / CC BY 4.0 licensed RIASEC instrument, legally reusable as a template.
- Item development was rigorous: each item passed seven screens (retranslation, sensitivity, comprehensibility, familiarity, training requirement, duplication, copyright).
- Published reliability benchmark: Cronbach alpha .78–.85 across the six RIASEC scales — a concrete external target to benchmark our own item bank against once piloted.
- Key lesson borrowed: write items as **concrete, relatable activities** ("Build kitchen cabinets," "Develop a new medicine," "Manage a retail store") rather than abstract trait statements ("I like analyzing problems"). This is the model for *content quality*.
- Primary sources: 
  - O*NET Interest Profiler Manual — https://www.onetcenter.org/dl_files/IP_Manual.pdf
  - Short Form Psychometric Characteristics summary — https://www.onetcenter.org/dl_files/IPSF_Psychometric.pdf
- Important caveat: O*NET itself is still a **Likert-rated** instrument (rate each activity 0-4, "strongly dislike" to "strongly like"), not forced-choice. It validates *item-writing style*, not our response-format decision.

### 7.2 Forced-choice / ipsative vocational assessment — validating the response format
- **Direct historical precedent for our exact audience**: the Vocational Interest Inventory (VII), based on Roe's interest areas, is an ipsative forced-choice inventory explicitly designed for counseling high school students with tentative vocational orientations (health sciences, engineering, business) — produced consistently interpretable profiles for that population.
- **Forced-choice generally outperforms single-scale (Likert) measures on predictive validity**: one study found quasi-ipsative FC measures had higher predictive validity for job performance than single-scale measures across several traits (e.g., conscientiousness .38 vs .22, emotional stability .20 vs .11).
- A comprehensive meta-analysis across nine occupational groups recommends quasi-ipsative forced-choice measures for personnel selection regardless of occupation group.
- Forced-choice is inherently more resistant to "gaming"/faking than Likert, since a student can't just rate every option highly — though faking still attenuates validity somewhat under lab conditions.
- **Important technical catch**: because forced-choice scores are relative (picking A means not picking B), raw score comparisons across students are not as straightforward as with Likert data — a phenomenon called "differential statement functioning" (a statement can carry different meaning/pull for different groups) is harder to detect in ipsative data. The modern fix is IRT-based scoring models built specifically for multidimensional forced-choice items — an active, current research area (key names: Stark, Drasgow, Chernyshenko). This connects directly to the adaptive-testing engine already planned — the same statistical machinery can likely solve both the adaptive-selection problem and the forced-choice scoring problem.

### 7.3 IPIP — sourcing the personality signal and the colleague's complementary instrument
- **International Personality Item Pool (IPIP)**: a public-domain, peer-reviewed item bank built around the Big Five (OCEAN) model, maintained by Lewis Goldberg and collaborators. Directly usable, legally, for both the personality signal (Section 2.4) and the aptitude/readiness layer more broadly.
- **Relevance to the personality signal**: gives a real citation trail for trait selection and item style, rather than inventing personality items from scratch.
- **Relevance to a colleague's complementary instrument**: a colleague independently built a separate 20-item scale classifying items into Interest, Abilities, Personality, and Values, grounded in Bandura's self-efficacy theory, Deci & Ryan's Self-Determination Theory (Academic Motivation Scale), and Super's Career Development Inventory. That instrument measures **post-decision readiness/confidence** (is an already-made choice well-grounded, is the student ready for university life) — a different question from ours (**which specialty should an undecided student consider**). Not a competing design: positioned as a natural **second-stage instrument**, run after a student has landed on a specialty (via our test or otherwise), to check motivation type and adjustment risk before enrollment. Worth proposing as a combined two-stage pipeline rather than two separate efforts.

### 7.4 Net assessment
Our converged design (concrete situational items + forced-choice format + adaptive delivery + separate aptitude layer) closely mirrors current best practice in vocational assessment research — arrived at independently rather than copied from a single existing model. One part has **no direct precedent in the literature**: pairing forced-choice interest items with a parallel disguised-aptitude layer, scored separately per specialty cluster. That combination appears genuinely novel — not a red flag, but it means there's no ready-made validation paper for it, so our own pilot with HIS students is the only evidence we'll have for whether that half works, and should be treated with proportionally more scrutiny than the RIASEC/forced-choice half.
